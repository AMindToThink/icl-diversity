"I've Seen How This Goes": Characterizing Diversity via Progressive Conditional Surprise
Download PDF

TL;DR: We design a measurement that uses in-context learning to evaluate the diversity of a set of responses, and we evaluate it on both human and LLM-generated data.
Abstract:
Measuring the diversity of creative outputs is central to evaluating post-training mode collapse, comparing decoding strategies, and quantifying creative behavior in both AI and human writing. We propose a new approach to measuring diversity using in-context learning, of which the ``Decan'' metric, 
, is the working instance we evaluate: a per-byte score read off the per-token log-probabilities of a base model 
 in a \emph{single forward pass} per permutation, with no embedding model, no reference corpus, and no human labels. This approach is grounded in information theory, makes use of language model in-context learning to detect a wide range of similarities between any number of inputs, and obviates the need to train a special-purpose model. The same pipeline scores AI samples and human-written response sets, with diversity treated as a property of (responses, prompt, scoring model). On Tevet and Berant's human-grounded McDiv benchmark, 
 reaches OCA 0.846 on the McDiv prompt_gen set where it performs best, behind the strongest neural baseline reported in Tevet and Berant (SentBERT, 0.897). On the OLMo-2-7B post-training pipeline, 
 drops monotonically across the base 
 SFT 
 DPO 
 RLVR stages, detecting the type of diversity loss that creative-writing applications care about.

Metareview:
(a) Summary.
DCaN scores a set of n continuations as Coherence (geometric-mean bits-per-byte given the prompt) times a_n (mean over permutations of the n-th continuation's bits-per-byte conditioned on the other n−1). It uses a base model's in-context learning as the similarity detector, with no embedding model, reference corpus, nor labels. The method was evaluated against human judgments on Tevet–Berant and on the OLMo-2-7B pipeline, where DCaN falls monotonically across base→SFT→DPO→RLVR.

(b) Strengths.
2bo8: mentions that the method id "simple and general", and that the authors are "forthright about possible limitations,", providing thoughtful appendix analyses with negative results, and high clarity.
pAJM: states that using ICL to measure diversity "is worth exploring... general and has the potential to provide a generally applicable measure."
5AF8: rates originality as "excellent."

(c) Weaknesses.

pAJM: DCaN "quite significantly underperforms SentBERT", which is a ~10× smaller model. Furthermore, it states that the "no embedding model, no labels" framing is "misleading" given the 3B judge.
2bo8: states that the paper provides essentially no sensitivity analysis (judge model, prompts, n, evaluated-model scale) and that the values are judge-relative with "no guidance for how to select such a model," making cross-work comparison impossible. Furthermore, the main results use only Qwen2.5-3B "despite its issues,".
pAJM: states that the protocol differs between Sections 4 and 5.
pAJM: states that the OLMo experiment assumes its ground truth (the diversity ordering) and never tests whether trivial features or coherence alone separate the checkpoints; no baseline metrics are run.
5AF8: mentions that there's no demonstrated advantage and that there is "little to no ablation of the actual metric."
2bo8: states that the the related works are relegated to the appendix and that the citations are light.
(d) Adjusting the odds.

the sensitivity suite both 2bo8 and pAJM specify with a judge sweep with selection guidance including n, permutations, length.
one consistent, justified protocol;
for OLMo, pAJM's requested comparison showing existing metrics fail where DCaN succeeds, or independent evidence for the ordering; 4. a clearly evidenced advantage over SentBERT on some axis.

The reviewers all rated our originality favorably, assigning it 3/4/3.

After submitting the paper, we did perform an experiment which demonstrates a case in which in-context learning diversity (in this case, the residual surprise a_n) is superior to SentBERT. When sentence share a structure but have different meanings and different words, in-context learning can pick up on the pattern while neither SentBERT nor n-grams can identify the pattern.

[AI] Concretely: every sentence in a set instantiates the same six-word part-of-speech pattern with freshly sampled words (e.g., "Pelicans coughed beyond trolls without senators."), while a matched control draws the same word classes but shuffles each sentence's order, making the control more diverse by a known ground truth of 0.12 bits/byte. Over 20 independent draws (Qwen2.5-3B judge, 40 responses per set), a_n separates the conditions with the correct sign and approximately the correct magnitude (gap 0.174 ± 0.053 bits/byte, p = 0.0024), while SentBERT shows no significant difference (p = 0.11) and distinct-n differs only through a capitalization artifact that vanishes on lowercased text (p = 0.88). We note transparently that the scalar D = C × a_n does not separate these two conditions: the scrambled control is genuinely less coherent, so C offsets its higher a_n as designed, and the structural-redundancy signal is read from the a_k curve and its endpoint a_n. [/AI]

Summary:
This work proposes a new metric DCaN which measures diversity and coherence. DCaN operates on a sample of n independently sampled continuations of a prompt p, generated by a human or AI, and is computed as the scalar product of Coherence and their diversity metric a_n. Coherence is simply the geometric mean of bits per byte for each continuation, conditioned on the prompt. a_n is the mean (over random permutations of continuations) bits per byte of the nth continuation, conditioned on all n-1 other continuations concatenated and appended to the prompt. This work measurea DCaN on Tevet–Berant Diversity-Eval, comparing DCaN values against human judgements. The work then shows DCaN scores on OLMo-2-7B to check for the expected decline over the course of the post-training pipline.

Contribution Type: General: Most submissions will fall into this type.
Strengths And Weaknesses:
Strengths

S1. The method is simple and general to various model architectures, tokenization methods, and human text.

S2. Authors are forthright about possible limitations and document some thoughtful consideration of the tradeoffs in designing such a metric. Appendices contain some thoughtful analyses with negative results.

S3. The paper is coherently written and has relatively high polish.

Weaknesses

W1. Main results are shown only with Qwen2.5-3B Base despite its issues, as the authors themselves note.

W2. No (or extremely limited) analysis is conducted of DCaN's sensitivity to base model choice, prompts, n (total continuations), generated (evaluated) model size and/or quality, etc.

W3. Because the DCaN values are a product of the judge model, absolute values (e.g., 0.19) have no meaning, only relative value with the same model. This makes comparisons between works impossible with the DCaN metric unless a judge model can be agreed upon, but because the authors do not analyse the behavior of the metric with respect to the judge model, there is no guidance for how to select such a model or model suite.

W4. (Smaller issue) Though it is common and understandable to relegate details to the appendix, the authors stretch this by even putting the related works in the appendix. It is difficult to judge the paper without reading at least 2-3 pages of the appendix.

W5. (Smaller issue) The paper is light on citations. For example, lack of diversity in creative tasks is now a well-documented phenomenon in LLMs and yet the authors have almost no citations about diversity in LLMs. Similarly, the related works section is very light, dedicating a small paragraph to the connection between perplexity and DCaN, but failing to include works directly related to generation diversity.

Quality: 2: not good
Clarity: 4: excellent
Significance: 2: not good
Originality: 3: good
Questions:
Q1. As noted in the limitations section, as well as Appendix C and E, the DCaN definition as the scalar product of C and a_n is not the only possible design for a metric to capture diversity and coherence. There are many ways to design a metric with the property of being high only if C and a_n are both high. Appendix E considers excess entropy, but because of the approximation of a_inf as a_n, the formulation C X E falls with the increase of a_n (if all else is fixed) and thus runs somewhat counter to the direction of C X a_n, which makes this a somewhat odd proposal. Were any other formulations considered?

Q2. Why are the Section 4 experiments carried out with Qwen2.5-3B Base despite its flip of incoherent vs coherent multi-mode? Is there more analysis about the specific dynamics introduced by different choices of judge (base) model, beyond the superficial observation that the base model matters? I saw the results on Qwen2.5-3B to Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base in Appendix H, but as you note, these were conducted before a bug fix and I largely disregard them, as you suggest treating them as only preliminary results.

Q3. You note in Appendix B.1 that you do not use a post-trained model for evaluation because it has "the kind of distributional bias we seek to avoid" and introduces "a confound between coherence-as-fluency and coherence-as539 alignment that the metric should not conflate". Did you conduct any experiments with an instruction tuned model as the base model to validate these concerns?

Q4. Do you have any experiments measuring the impact of the number of permutations (beyond only 3 vs 100), the choice of n, and other hyperparameters of the evaluation process? I see experiments in C.3 about the impact of # of modes in your synthetic dataset, and a discussion in C.5 about 3 vs 100 permutations, but nothing else.

Q5. Do you have example continuation texts corresponding to 5, demonstrating the decrease in diversity indicated by the DCaN metric?

Limitations:
Yes, the authors are upfront about the limitations of their work, and I cover in the questions and weaknesses my questions about any additional concerns.

Summary:
This paper proposes a metric called Decan to measure the diversity of language content, including human written and LLM generated. The metric is a product between a coherence measure (inversed perplexity) and a surprise measure. Both elements are evaluated using a base language model, different from the one that generates the language content. The authors conduct experiments on human-written content and LLM generated content.

Contribution Type: General: Most submissions will fall into this type.
Strengths And Weaknesses:
Strengths:

The approach of using in-context learning to measure diversity is worth exploring. It is general and has the potential to provide a generally applicable measure of diversity/surprise.
Weakness:

I believe the most important aspects of this paper worth improving is (1) a consistent and clear protocol for measuring the proposed Decan metric, (2) sufficient empirical evidence that the metric strongly aligns with human measurements of diversity and (3) sufficient ablation studies that shows the measurement is well behaved when we change hyper parameters of the measurement protocol, such as how many responses per prompt, length of the responses, with chat format or not, and more. I provide more concrete suggestions for improvement below.

The authors are encouraged to define a consistent and clear protocol for measurement. I noticed the measurement is conducted differently in Section 4 and 5. In Section 5, response truncation is applied: “we truncate every (stage, prompt) tuple’s responses to a common per-prompt UTF-8 byte length before scoring” . This truncation choice seems to be post-hoc and leaves no justification/guidance on whether others should truncate or not when doing this measurement.

The empirical evidence does not support Decan is a strong diversity metric. From Table 2, experiment on Tevet–Berant dataset, it quite significantly underperforms SentBERT. SentBERT is also much smaller (300M I think? Authors can correct me if wrong) while Qwen2.5-3B is 10x larger.

Experiment result on OLMo in Section 5 provides weak and loose empirical support to the utility of the metric. --- It takes a loose assumption that the diversity decreases from Base model, to SFT, DPO, RLVR. The authors should provide justification/evidence/measurement for this, especially because this is used as the ground-truth for the Decan measurement. --- Second, it is unclear how to interpret the result that Decan separates these model checkpoints. What if separating these model checkpoints is a very easy task, and Decan only weakly measures diversity but it is sufficient to separate these model checkpoints? What if many other features (such as coherence) can also be used to separate these model checkpoints, and the coherence term in Decan simply can tell these model checkpoints apart? The experiment needs to be better designed. Comparing with existing methods on this experiment as well is helpful, for example if the results shows Decan can separate models apart but other methods cannot.

I believe for designing an metric for diversity, ablation experiments are necessary to show that it behaves well / is robust to common hyper parameters to the measurement protocol. For example, this includes length of the response, length of the question, number of responses, different base models, different templates, human and AI written. Better understanding on these dimensions will guide how to apply and interpret the measurement.

By “reference corpus” we were referring to the human-labeled datasets such as the SNLI, MultiNLI, and STS-Benchmark datasets used to train Sentence-BERT. While empirically Sentence-BERT generalizes well from these datasets, we developed Decan in part because we disliked the idea of relying on the model to generalize from a limited dataset. By using base model in-context learning, we instead rely on the model to generalize from a vast internet-scale dataset of patterns. We concede that SentBERT (.3B parameters) is much smaller than Qwen2.5-3B. [AI] We concede that "reference corpus" was imprecise: none of the benchmark's metrics consult a corpus at evaluation time, and we meant these supervised training corpora. In the revised version we will state this as "no task-specific supervised training data." [/AI]

"Auxiliary models" refers to the process of training special-purpose similarity models. Both neural baselines in Table 2 fall into this category. In-context learning diversity metrics avoid this step by using a general purpose pretrained model.

The motivation of measuring diversity has a different set of requirements than the database-scale similarity metrics like Sentence-BERT. For applications such as performing reinforcement learning for creative-writing, it is important that a diversity metric can withstand optimization pressure and more closely tracks the thing we want to optimize, and we argue that we have reasons to expect in-context learning diversity metrics will track this better, though we left this for future work. Distinct-n, in contrast, would assign random noise the highest diversity score. Decan avoids that pitfall. The speed and memory usage of the algorithm is less important for this use case.

We tested the "assumption that the diversity decreases from Base model, to SFT, DPO, RLVR" by measuring the correlation between Decan and existing metrics and found that they correlated strongly (Appendix I).

Although the paper does not report whether coherence alone can separate the checkpoints, we did check whether coherence alone can classify the ConTest dataset (Table 2). It performed much worse than Decan, so we used Decan for further experiments. Additionally, in unreported numbers on the OLMo experiments, we find that coherence rises rather than falls during post-training.

[AI] On the length-matched AlpacaEval subset, mean coherence C rises from 0.436 at base to 0.674-0.698 across the post-training stages (SFT 0.674, DPO 0.694, RLVR 0.698), while D falls. The separation is carried by the a_n term. Coherence alone therefore cannot separate the checkpoints; it moves in the opposite direction. [/AI]

[AI] On the Section 4 vs Section 5 protocol difference: the truncation in Section 5 is stated with its justification in the Setup paragraph. Per-byte cross-entropy in a causal LM decreases with within-response context, so comparing conditions whose response lengths differ systematically requires a fixed byte denominator; we therefore truncate each (stage, prompt) tuple's responses to a common per-prompt byte length. Section 4 does not truncate because the rule only bites when the compared sets differ systematically in length.[/AI] Practitioners can length-match when they want to measure diversity in a fixed window, and they can skip length-matching if they want to measure overall surprisal. Both to have their uses, though we expect length-matching to be overall more informative.

[AI] On how to choose the base model: re-scoring with judges from three model families (124M to 8B parameters) left the human-label correlations essentially unchanged (conTest prompt_gen Spearman rho 0.54-0.58 across judges), so rankings are not sensitive to the choice among models.
