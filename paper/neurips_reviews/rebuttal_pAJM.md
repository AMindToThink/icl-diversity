[claude-scaffold: answers pAJM Questions ¶1 — the "no reference corpus... misleading" critique and "what existing methods require reference corpus?"]

pAJM: By “reference corpus” we were referring to the datasets such as the SNLI, MultiNLI, and STS-Benchmark datasets used to train Sentence-BERT. We apologize for not making that clear. While empirically Sentence-BERT generalizes well from these datasets, we developed Decan in part because we disliked the idea of relying on the model to generalize from a limited dataset. By using base model in-context learning, we instead rely on the model to generalize from a vast internet-scale dataset of patterns. We concede that SentBERT (.3B parameters) is much smaller than Qwen2.5-3B.

**[AI]** You also asked which existing methods require human labels or auxiliary classifiers: both neural baselines in Table 2 do. BERT-STS is an auxiliary similarity model, "a BERT model fine-tuned on Semantic Textual Similarity" pairs "annotated with scores from 1-5 denoting their semantic similarity" (Tevet and Berant's description), and SentBERT's training data is likewise human-annotated (entailment labels, then similarity scores). We concede that "reference corpus" was imprecise: none of the benchmark's metrics consult a corpus at evaluation time, and we meant these supervised training corpora. In the revised version we will state the distinction precisely as "no task-specific supervised training data." **[/AI]**

[claude-scaffold: answers pAJM Questions ¶1 continued — "be more specific on their method's advantages... is Decan faster, requires less compute?"]

The motivation of measuring diversity has a different set of requirements than the database-scale similarity metrics like Sentence-BERT. For applications such as performing reinforcement learning for creative-writing, it is important that a diversity metric can withstand optimization pressure and more closely tracks the thing we want to optimize, and we argue that we have reasons to expect in-context learning diversity metrics will track this better, though we left this for future work. Distinct-n, in contrast, would assign random noise the highest diversity score, and Decan avoids that pitfall. The speed and memory usage of the algorithm is less important for this use case.

You asked about the "assumption that the diversity decreases from Base model, to SFT, DPO, RLVR". We didn't just assume it, we also measured the correlation between Decan and existing metrics and found that they correlated strongly (Appendix I).

[claude-scaffold: answers pAJM Weakness ¶3 continued — "What if many other features (such as coherence) can also be used to separate these model checkpoints"]

pAJM: Although the paper does not report whether coherence alone can separate the checkpoints, we did check whether coherence alone can classify the ConTest dataset (Table 2). Seeing that it performed much worse than Decan, we used Decan for further experiments. Additionally, in unreported numbers on the OLMo experiments, we find that coherence rises rather than falls during post-training while D falls.

**[AI]** On the length-matched AlpacaEval subset, mean coherence C rises from 0.436 at base to 0.674-0.698 across the post-training stages (SFT 0.674, DPO 0.694, RLVR 0.698), while D falls from 0.481 to 0.281-0.286. The separation is carried by the a_n term, which drops from 1.142 at base to 0.419-0.512 post-training. Coherence alone therefore cannot explain the checkpoint separation; it moves in the opposite direction. **[/AI]**

[claude-scaffold: proposed answer to pAJM Weakness ¶2 — protocol / truncation. AI-drafted.]

**[AI]** On the Section 4 vs Section 5 protocol difference: the truncation in Section 5 is stated with its justification in the Setup paragraph, its footnote, and the Limitations section. Per-byte cross-entropy in a causal LM decreases with within-response context, so comparing conditions whose response lengths differ systematically requires a fixed byte denominator; we therefore truncate each (stage, prompt) tuple's responses to a common per-prompt byte length. Section 4 does not truncate because the rule only bites when the compared sets differ systematically in length. The general decision procedure: hold n, the judge model, and the conditioning format fixed within a comparison, and length-match exactly when the compared sets differ systematically in response length. **[/AI]**

[claude-scaffold: proposed answers to pAJM Question ¶2 (base-model choice), Question ¶3 (a_k vs k), Question ¶4 (lines 153-158). AI-drafted.]

**[AI]** On how to choose the base model: re-scoring with judges from three model families (124M to 8B parameters) left the human-label correlations essentially unchanged (conTest prompt_gen Spearman rho 0.54-0.58 across judges), so rankings are not sensitive to the choice among reasonably-trained base models. Cross-work comparability then comes from pinning a public base-model checkpoint as the reference judge, the same mechanism by which embedding metrics are comparable across papers only when they share a pinned public encoder. On a_k versus k: we agree the curve is the more informative object, and the paper says so in several places (Section 3, Appendix B, Appendix C.6): the a_k curve is the primary object of the framework, and D_{Ca_n} is a deliberately simple, comparable scalar summary for settings that need a single number; full curves are reported in Appendix C.3 and Section 5. On lines 153-158: we apologize for the confusing wording. The passage discloses a construction confound in McDiv_nuggets, and the confound works against our diversity term: the low-diversity sets are paraphrases of self-selected dramatic endings, which raises their unconditional surprise a_1 in the direction opposite the label. By k = n the shared structure dominates, so a_n is nevertheless lower for the low-diversity sets. The same confound spuriously helps the C term on this benchmark (dramatic endings read as less probable to the judge), and the sentence you quote is our disclosure of exactly that, so that readers do not over-credit C. Appendix F documents the mechanism and shows the gap persists within response-length bins (collapsing only in the longest bin), so it is a content effect rather than a length artifact. **[/AI]**
