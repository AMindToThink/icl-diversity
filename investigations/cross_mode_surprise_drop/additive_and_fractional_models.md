## Original prose

### The independent (additive) model fails

If cross-mode contributions were independent, summing pairwise contributions should predict the observed $a_k$ curve. Figure 5 compared the predicted and observed cumulative drops at $m=10$: the independent model overpredicts by $\approx 10.6\times$ (predicted-bits macro vs.\ observed-bits macro). The first few responses provide most of the format/topic calibration; subsequent responses have rapidly diminishing marginal value.

**Figure 5 (removed).** `predicted_vs_observed_m10.png` — three-panel figure: (i) predicted vs.\ observed $a_k$ curve at $m=10$, with additive and two fractional model traces; (ii) marginal drops per step; (iii) prediction accuracy (observed / predicted) per step. Caption: "Predicted (from pairwise matrix, assuming independence) vs.\ observed cumulative surprise drop at $m=10$. The independent model overpredicts, demonstrating that pairwise contributions are not additive: information saturates rapidly with context length."

### A fractional model gets the shape right but not the scale

The additive model's failure suggests a different framing: each response should resolve a *fraction* of remaining uncertainty rather than a fixed number of bits. The pairwise matrix gives us these fractions: $r_{\text{off}} = \bar M_{\text{off}} / a_1$ (cross-mode) and $r_{\text{diag}} = \bar M_{\text{diag}} / a_1$ (same-mode). With an irreducible floor $a_\infty$ (estimated independently from the $m=1$ asymptote), the recurrence is:

$$a_{k+1} = a_\infty + (a_k - a_\infty)(1 - r_k), \qquad r_k = r_{\text{off}} + p_{\text{rep}}(k) \cdot (r_{\text{diag}} - r_{\text{off}})$$

where $p_{\text{rep}}(k)$ is the probability that position $k{+}1$ shares a mode with some position in $1 \ldots k$.

This model produces exponentially decaying marginals (matching observations) rather than the additive model's increasing marginals. At $m=1$, it is nearly exact. However, it predicts approximately the same total drop ($\sim 130$ bits) for all $m$ values, while the observed total drop ranges from the $m=1$ bits-macro to the $m=10$ bits-macro — a several-fold overprediction at $m=10$.

The core limitation is that the pairwise matrix measures *single-encounter* information transfer. Real $a_k$ curves involve repeated encounters with overlapping information, where each subsequent encounter has diminishing marginal returns. A complete model would need to account for information overlap between responses, structure that the pairwise matrix does not capture. What the pairwise matrix *does* successfully explain is: (1) the first step at each $m$ (by construction — calibration, not prediction), (2) why Qwen drops and GPT-2 doesn't (the sign of the off-diagonal; the fractional model is not needed for this — the additive expected-first-step-drop argument in the paper already makes this point), and (3) the qualitative exponential shape.

## Honest retrospective

The additive model was a strawman — information-theoretic intuition already says pairwise MI shouldn't decompose additively, so falsifying it is cheap and unsurprising. Its $10.6\times$ overprediction number doesn't characterize the real dynamics, only quantifies how badly the trivial null fails.

The fractional model is more defensible — it gets the qualitative shape right and has a clean negative-finding interpretation (single-encounter pairwise measurements can't capture multi-step information overlap). But its three "successful explanations" are mostly weak: (1) is calibration by construction, (2) is really explained by the sign of the off-diagonal without any model, (3) is weak because most saturating processes produce exponential-looking curves. And the scale failure dilutes the message.

Neither model is load-bearing for the paper's thesis that progressive surprise is a diversity metric. The question Section 8.3 actually needs to answer — "why does Qwen show exponential decay rather than the predicted sigmoid?" — is resolved by the sign-of-off-diagonal argument in the retained bullet, without either model.
